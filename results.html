Results

What we found

We ran every method on 136 graphs: families we generated to test one thing at a time, real circuits from a standard benchmark suite, and graphs built to be hard for Howard's method. Here is what held up, and where our idea doesn't help.

Experiment E1

Every method gives the same answer

Skipping is only worth having if it never changes the answer. Every method returns exact fractions, so “the same” means equal, not close. On every graph where all methods finished, they agreed on every state's gain, and on the small graphs we also checked them against brute force: trying every policy there is.

Experiment E3

Every skip was safe

For each SCC the cascade skipped, we ran Karp's algorithm on it anyway and checked that no cycle inside beat leaving. None did. That is what the soundness proof promises; this checks the code does what the proof says.

It doesn't skip everything it could. On graphs of many small SCCs it skipped about three in four of the SCCs that could have gone, and on SCCs with long best cycles only a handful.

Experiment E2

Where skipping helps, and where it doesn't

The share of Karp's work the cascade avoided, by kind of graph. Skipping can only happen when an SCC's best cycle is no better than somewhere you can go next, so it pays when graphs get better further along, and not at all when they get worse.

Experiment E4

The cheapest test does most of the skipping

The cascade first compares the largest reward inside an SCC with its exit value, which costs one look at each edge. Only if that fails does it build Karp rows and try δk. The first test alone accounts for nearly every skip. Numbers are SCCs, summed over every graph of each kind.

Experiment E7

Why δk stalls on long cycles

δk is the most any state's best k-step reward grows from one row to the next. When the best cycle is long, those rewards grow in bursts, once per trip round the cycle, so the largest single step stays well above the average and δk never comes down to it. It depends on the cycles, not the SCC: add a self-loop as good as the best cycle and the stall goes.

Experiment E6

On real circuits, and on graphs built to be hard

How the time grows

Both axes are logarithmic. On this family, doubling the size multiplies Howard's time by about 14 to 16, and the hybrid's by about 7 to 8.

Where the time goes

Why the cascade is slow on circuits

We timed each phase of each method on the 36 circuits. Most circuit SCCs have no cycle at all (95% of them), so there is little left to skip, and almost all the cascade's time is Karp on what remains: a median 92%.

The surprise was where inside Karp. The final formula, not building the table, takes a median 85% of the cascade's time, because every step builds an exact fraction and reduces it. A cheaper exact comparison, tried outside the frozen code, made the cascade about 4× faster and cut Howard's median lead on these circuits from 12.5× to 3.2×. Howard still won on all 36.

For the hybrid, about 42% of its time goes on turning per-SCC answers into one policy for the whole graph, a step Howard doesn't need. Trying δk before Howard (option B) rarely helps: a median 5% difference on circuits, and up to 24.6× slower when the rows go to waste.

What these results don't say

  • Times are from one machine (an Apple M2 with 16 GB), the fastest of three runs. Ratios between methods are more reliable than the times themselves.
  • Our Karp has a constant-factor cost we found late, so part of Howard's lead on circuits is our implementation, not the algorithms.
  • The generated families were chosen to show when skipping can and can't help. They are not a sample of real-world graphs.
  • The five largest graphs ran only Howard and the hybrid: Karp's table for them doesn't fit in 16 GB of memory.
